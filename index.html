Abstract

Robot imitation learning relies on expert demonstrations, yet collecting complete trajectories is labor-intensive and training on every transition is computationally costly. Most pipelines allocate both budgets at the trajectory level, overlooking the distinct spatial and dynamic characteristics of task phases. We present the Trajectory Saliency Detector (TSD), a training-free method that identifies precise and agile phases directly from demonstration kinematics. TSD aligns demonstrations, detects precise operations from cross-demonstration pose dispersion, and locates agile maneuvers using centripetal acceleration. The detected phases support dataset compression by combining complete base demonstrations with salient source segments, and dataset expansion by collecting local demonstrations at identified phases. Across five simulated tasks with two policy architectures, TSD uses 25.4--32.0% fewer transitions and reduces policy-training time by 13.4--26.4% relative to the corresponding complete datasets. Its average success remains within 1.5 percentage points of full-data performance or exceeds it, while outperforming all comparable-budget baselines. Across three real-world tasks, TSD-guided collection outperforms matched-budget complete-trajectory collection, approaches full-data performance, and reduces collection time by 15.2%. These results show that phase-level trajectory saliency enables more efficient allocation of demonstration and training resources.

Method

Detecting salient trajectory phases

Precise

Cross-demonstration pose convergence

After pose-aware temporal alignment, TSD measures local end-effector pose dispersion across demonstrations. Concentrated, low-speed regions provide evidence of spatially constrained operations such as grasping and placement.

Agile

Dynamic response with geometric verification

Centripetal acceleration captures rapid changes in motion direction. RDP points and trajectory tortuosity provide geometric verification that suppresses derivative-induced spurious peaks.
